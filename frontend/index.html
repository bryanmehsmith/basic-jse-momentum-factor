JSE Momentum Factor

← Back to all demos

Momentum is the observation that stocks which have performed well over the trailing several months to a year tend to keep performing well over the following months, and stocks which have lagged tend to keep lagging. This walks through the classic "12-1" construction, why it's built the way it is, what turning it into a portfolio actually means, and lets you backtest it long-only on the JSE with your own settings. Look for Technical detail panels wherever you want the exact formulas.

How momentum works

Nobody fully agrees on why it works, and the standard explanations are behavioral rather than risk-based. Investors are slow to fully price in new information, an earnings surprise, a change in fundamentals, so the price keeps drifting toward fair value over months rather than adjusting all at once (underreaction). As a price move becomes visible, more investors pile in and reinforce the trend for a while (herding). And investors tend to sell winners quickly while holding onto losers far too long, which suppresses a stock's price reaction and lets an existing trend persist longer than it otherwise would (the disposition effect). These effects fade, and eventually reverse, over multi-year horizons, which is exactly why a momentum strategy defines a specific formation window rather than just "the stock has always gone up."

Technical detail

Momentum is one of the most persistent, widely documented anomalies in equity markets, first formalized by Jegadeesh & Titman (1993) using US stocks, and since replicated across most markets and time periods, including the JSE. It sits awkwardly next to the efficient-markets view: the behavioral explanations above are the common account, but there's an ongoing academic argument over whether some of the premium instead compensates for a real, hard-to-diversify risk (crash risk, see the risks section below) rather than being pure mispricing. Both views agree on the empirical pattern; they disagree on why it should be expected to persist.

The "12-1" construction

The classic momentum signal ranks stocks by their return over the trailing 12 months, but deliberately skips the most recent month. That's not an oversight: returns over the last month tend to reverse (a stock that jumped last month is somewhat more likely to give some of it back), which works against the momentum effect rather than for it. Skipping it keeps the ranking about the trend, not last month's noise.

Technical detail

For a formation window of F months and a skip of S months, the score at month t is \(\text{score}(t) = \dfrac{P(t-S)}{P(t-S-F)} - 1\), i.e. the cumulative return from t - S - F to t - S. With the classic S = 1, F = 12 ("12-1"), that's the return from 13 months ago to 1 month ago, not 12 months ago to today. The sliders below let you move both F and S independently.

Turning the signal into a portfolio

Once every stock has a momentum score, the ranked list is split into buckets (quantiles). The textbook momentum strategy goes long the top bucket (past winners) and short the bottom bucket (past losers): a "long-short" or "winners-minus-losers" portfolio that's roughly market-neutral. Shorting individual JSE stocks isn't practical for most participants, so this demo instead runs a long-only top-quantile portfolio: equal-weight the top-ranked names, rebalance every month, hold until the next rebalance. That means it is not market-neutral: it carries the market's own ups and downs on top of whatever momentum itself contributes. It's also worth being honest that every monthly rebalance would incur real trading costs (spreads, commissions, market impact) that this backtest doesn't model, so the numbers below are a ceiling, not what an investor would actually keep.

Technical detail

At each rebalance date, holdings = the top \(\max\!\big(1,\ \lfloor n \times \text{quantile} \rfloor\big)\) names by score, equal-weighted. The portfolio's return for that month is the mean of those names' next-month returns. This is a vectorized, unmodeled backtest: no transaction costs, no slippage, no bid/ask spread, no capacity constraints.

What these numbers mean

Sharpe ratio: \(\text{Sharpe} = \dfrac{\overline{r - r_f}}{\sigma_{r - r_f}}\sqrt{12}\), the mean monthly excess return over the risk-free rate divided by its standard deviation, annualized. Higher is more return per unit of risk taken.

Annualized return: the constant yearly rate that would produce the same total growth over the sample.

Max drawdown: the largest peak-to-trough decline in cumulative value over the sample; a rough sense of the worst it got.

Cumulative return: total growth over the whole sample, un-annualized.

Risks to know going in

Momentum crashes

Momentum is prone to sharp, sudden reversals. In a market rebound after a crash, yesterday's beaten-down "losers" can rally hardest, hurting a long-winners position (and badly hurting the classic long-winners/short-losers version) right when it's least convenient.

A small, coarse universe

The JSE has far fewer liquid large-caps than a market like the S&P 500, so quantile buckets here are coarse and results are more sensitive to a handful of individual stocks than a backtest on a deeper universe would be.

Unmodeled costs

Every monthly rebalance in real life means trading: spreads, commissions, market impact. None of that is in this backtest, so treat the numbers above as a ceiling on what a real implementation could achieve, not a forecast.
